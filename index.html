Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

WACC stands for Weighted Average Cost of Capital. It represents the average return a company must pay to all its investors — both debt holders and equity shareholders — for using their funds.

It's used as the discount rate to evaluate investments and projects.

Formula

WACC = (E/V) × Re + (D/V) × Rd × (1 − T)

  • E = Market value of equity
  • D = Market value of debt
  • V = Total capital (E + D)
  • Re = Cost of equity
  • Rd = Cost of debt
  • T = Tax rate

The Problem with Normal WACC

Companies usually calculate one overall WACC. For example, a company's WACC might be 10%. But not all projects have the same risk.

Project Comparison

  • Project A: Low risk, stable returns
  • Project B: High risk, uncertain returns

Using 10% for both could lead to poor decisions — because each project carries a different level of risk.

→ Solution: Risk-Adjusted WACC

Risk-Adjusted WACC modifies the standard WACC to reflect the specific risk of a project.

Higher project risk → Higher discount rate
Lower project risk → Lower discount rate

Why Adjust WACC?

Because investors expect higher returns for higher risk. Ignoring this leads to valuation mistakes:

  • If WACC is too low: Cash flows seem larger → You might accept a bad project 😬
  • If WACC is too high: Cash flows seem smaller → You might reject a good project ❌

How to Calculate Risk-Adjusted WACC

The main adjustment happens in the Cost of Equity (Re).

The CAPM Formula: Re = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf)

  • Rf: Risk-free rate
  • β (Beta): Measure of project's risk
  • Rm − Rf: Market risk premium

Understanding Beta

BetaMeaning
β = 1Average market risk
β < 1Lower risk
β > 1Higher risk

Example 1

Risk-free rate: 4%    Market premium: 6%

For Project 1 (β = 1): Re = 4 + (1×6) = 10%

For Project 2 (β = 1.5): Re = 4 + (1.5×6) = 13%

Higher Beta → Higher Cost of Equity → Higher WACC.

Risk-Adjusted WACC Example

  • Equity = 60%
  • Debt = 40%
  • Cost of Debt = 8%
  • Tax = 30%
  • Cost of Equity (risky project) = 13%

Equity portion = 60% × 13% = 7.8%

Debt portion = 40% × 8% × (1 − 0.30) = 2.24%

WACC = 7.8 + 2.24 = 10.04%

Decision Example

Project IRR = 9%
Normal WACC (8%) → Accept ✅
Risk-Adjusted WACC (10.04%) → Reject ❌

When to Use Risk-Adjusted WACC

  • New business ventures or markets
  • High-risk or uncertain projects
  • International expansions (country risk)
  • Technology/innovation projects

Advantages

  • More accurate project valuation
  • Better investment choices
  • Reflects actual project risk
  • Prevents over/undervaluation mistakes

Limitations

  • Estimating beta accurately is challenging
  • Future market conditions are uncertain
  • Poor estimates lead to unreliable WACC

Easy Analogy

Think of lending money. Would you charge the same interest to a trusted friend and a stranger with bad credit? Of course not! Riskier borrower → Higher interest rate. That's exactly what Risk-Adjusted WACC does for projects.

Final Definition

Risk-Adjusted WACC is the weighted average cost of capital adjusted to reflect a project's specific risk level, ensuring accurate investment evaluation and better decisions.

The Concept Chain

Project Risk → Beta → Cost of Equity → WACC → Investment Decision
